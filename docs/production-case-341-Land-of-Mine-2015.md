# Production Case 341: Land of Mine (2015)

Status: **fully integrated and source verified**

Scenario ID: `scenario_land_of_mine_2015`

## Production identity

Martin Zandvliet's Danish-German historical drama concentrates the aftermath of the Second World War into one West Jutland beach, one repeated mine-clearing procedure and one changing relationship between Danish Sergeant Carl Rasmussen and a group of young German prisoners. The case is not modelled as conventional battle cinema. Its production logic is a postwar moral chamber drama built from researched history, exposed landscape, youthful bodies, procedural suspense and the gradual collapse of revenge as a stable directing position.

The production system joins:

- several years of historical research into Denmark's suppressed mine-clearing history
- a restricted sergeant-and-prisoners viewpoint
- Oksbøl and West Jutland coast locations
- a Danish-German co-production and mixed-national crew
- daylight exteriors contrasting summer beauty with bunkers, mines and injury
- many newly cast German youth performers and Roland Møller's first leading role
- military and mine-procedure rehearsal
- an actor-following camera influenced by 1960s cinema and the Maysles brothers
- sea, wind, breathing, metal and silence as procedural sound
- safety-controlled pyrotechnics, special effects, stunt work and digital assistance

## Storyboard and directing decisions

The case treats the beach as both historical landscape and suspense machine. Wide daylight views establish beauty, exposure and the scale of the task; actor-following proximity transfers attention to hands, breath, rods, detonators and glances. Repetition is essential: the same crawl-and-probe action must remain legible enough that small deviations, fatigue or interrupted concentration become dangerous.

Carl's moral change is staged through access and behaviour rather than explanatory speeches. Blocking, food, shelter, distance and permission gradually reorganize the relation between guard and prisoners. The production must therefore preserve the beach grid, barracks geography and chain of command as carefully as the emotional arc.

## Schedule decisions

A production plan derived from the verified system would separate:

1. **Beach procedure:** daylight mine-grid work, actor rehearsal, continuity of markers, wind and tide, close hand inserts and safety supervision.
2. **Barracks and Oksbøl interiors:** hunger, discipline, sleep, treatment and group relations with a reduced effects footprint.
3. **Farm and human-contact material:** Carl's contact with the surrounding Danish world and the conflict between civilian life and the mined coast.
4. **Controlled detonations and aftermath:** pyrotechnic windows, stunt and medical-effects teams, locked safety zones, matched plates and restrained digital cleanup.

Weather, daylight continuity and beach access are primary scheduling constraints. Effects days must be built around safety and geography rather than actor availability alone.

## Budget decisions

The model prioritizes:

- historical uniforms, props, bunkers, barracks and mine-field dressing
- location access, weather cover and coastal logistics
- youth-cast rehearsal and procedural training
- dedicated armoury, mine, stunt, pyrotechnic and medical-effects supervision
- multiple controlled explosion units and reset capacity
- sound capture and postproduction for metal detail, breath, wind, sea and detonation dynamics
- restrained visual-effects cleanup and safety integration rather than spectacle expansion
- Danish-German co-production administration, travel and language support

The case does not claim an audited historical production budget. Its budget lesson is structural: a limited number of locations can still require a high safety, rehearsal and reset allocation when suspense depends on actors working at ground level around simulated explosives.

## Production design and location system

The Oksbøl coast is not a generic beach. Pale sand, open sea, dunes, concrete bunkers, black markers and narrow cleared paths create a graphic system where beauty and danger coexist in every frame. Production design must preserve the historical task as visible labour: the grid, tools, collected mines, barracks and military hierarchy explain the action before dialogue does.

Most scenes remain in daylight. This makes weather and exposure part of the design, while the contrast between bright summer surface and dark human purpose supplies the central visual contradiction.
