Verification date: **2026-08-14**

Canonical scenario: `scenario_the_lonely_villa_1909`

## Why this film is a Production Case

*The Lonely Villa* fills the final P0 gap in Chapter 3, **From views to stories**. Its distinct production problem is not simply “cut faster.” The film must keep **separated spaces and simultaneous actions intelligible over repeated alternation** while increasing suspense toward one rescue.

The playable system tracks three action lines:

1. the family trapped inside the villa;
2. burglars forcing entry;
3. the husband moving from a disabled automobile toward police-assisted rescue.

The telephone makes distance narratively active. It allows husband and family to share one emergency while occupying different spaces; when the connection is lost, the rupture raises both dramatic danger and the formal pressure on the alternation.

## Production and material evidence

### Library of Congress

The Library of Congress preserves the film and paper-print material and identifies it as a 1909 Biograph production. Its record supplies D. W. Griffith direction, Mack Sennett and Stanner E. V. Taylor writing, G. W. Bitzer and Arthur Marvin camera credits, the adaptation from André de Lorde's *Au Téléphone*, production number 3565, copyright date and filming dates.

The LOC record also anchors the mixed production geography: **Fort Lee, New Jersey and Biograph's New York City studio**. That evidence is used in the playable production-design problem so recurring action spaces are not treated as abstract editing boxes.

### AFI Catalog

AFI independently records the 10 June 1909 release, Biograph production/distribution, Griffith direction, Bitzer/Marvin photography, approximately 750-foot silent black-and-white format, Fort Lee/New York locations and the detailed rescue synopsis.

AFI also preserves a contemporary *Moving Picture World* response emphasizing how strongly the suspense affected audiences. This supports reception history without turning later canonical reputation into the only evidence of significance.

### Museum of Modern Art

MoMA independently catalogs and preserves *The Lonely Villa* as a 1909 Griffith moving-image work, giving the case a separate institutional identity/survival check.

## Scholarly control

### Tom Gunning — *Screen*

Gunning's article **“Heard over the phone: The Lonely Villa and the de Lorde tradition of the terrors of technology”** treats the film specifically in relation to telephone melodrama. This supports the production model in which communication technology and parallel narrative are one dramatic system.

### Joyce E. Jesionowski — University of California Press

*Thinking in Pictures: Dramatic Structure in D. W. Griffith's Biograph Films* is used as a historiographic safeguard. UC Press explicitly frames Griffith as both pioneer and mythmaker and notes that devices often credited to him, including intercutting, were already practiced internationally.

### Tom Gunning — University of Illinois Press

*D.W. Griffith And The Origins Of American Narrative Film: The Early Years at Biograph* places Griffith's first Biograph years within a wider transformation of film production, audience relations and narrative organization. The playable case therefore models **consolidation and intensification**, not isolated invention.

## Historiographic safeguards

The Production Case must not state or imply that:

- Griffith invented cross-cutting;
- *The Lonely Villa* was the first film to alternate between simultaneous actions;
- earlier British, French or American experiments are merely primitive steps toward Griffith;
- later classical shot-reverse-shot coverage should be retrofitted onto the 1909 production;
- synchronized telephone voices or effects were part of the original photographed production.

Instead, the case teaches a demonstrable historical achievement: **sustained alternation among separated action lines becomes a dependable engine of suspense, spatial comprehension and narrative convergence**.
