## What is verified

The Production Case is built around claims supported by institutional film-history and preservation sources:

1. **Feature-scale production.** The original work ran for more than an hour and was distributed across roughly five reels, making duration, reel handling, programme structure and sustained dramatic organization central production problems.
2. **Charles Tait direction.** NFSA/UNESCO/BFI records identify Charles Tait with the 1906 film.
3. **Producer-exhibitor organization.** The production belonged to the Tait/Johnson/Gibson commercial exhibition world rather than to a modern studio/auteur model.
4. **Location production.** NFSA records chiefly associate filming with the Veitch family estate at Heidelberg outside Melbourne.
5. **Touring and exhibition.** The film became a touring commercial attraction; feature scale is therefore treated as an exhibition/distribution problem as well as a narrative one.
6. **Silent photographed film versus live presentation.** NFSA records lecturer commentary and behind-the-scenes/live sound effects as exhibition practices. They are not encoded as synchronized original production sound.
7. **Fragmentary survival.** Only fragments of the original survive. Modern reconstruction/restoration is used as preservation evidence and is not represented as an intact surviving 1906 print.

## Preservation and historiography safeguards

The Production Case permanently avoids four misleading claims:

- It does **not** say that an intact complete original survives.
- It does **not** describe lecturer commentary or live effects as synchronized production sound.
- It does **not** claim that Charles Tait alone invented the feature film.
- It does **not** claim that Australia alone invented feature-length narrative cinema.

Instead, the case teaches *The Story of the Kelly Gang* as a major landmark in a broader international transition toward feature-length narrative production and exhibition.
