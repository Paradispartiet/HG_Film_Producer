## Individual film article

Kiyoshi Kurosawa places a supernatural story within the early consumer internet of Japan. His 2005 Reverse Shot interview explains that he wrote the film while the internet was spreading rapidly and its social consequences remained unfamiliar. The ordinary computer becomes a dramatic threshold, not merely an exotic prop. The story's loneliness is a fictional and aesthetic inquiry, not an empirical prediction that internet use necessarily causes isolation. ([reverse](https://reverseshot.org/interviews/entry/1503/kiyoshi-kurosawa), [eye](https://www.eyefilm.nl/en/whats-on/pulse/1597515), [bfi](https://player.bfi.org.uk/subscription/film/watch-pulse-2001-online))

The film's material texture matters as much as its premise. Brian Hu's UCLA essay identifies the muted initial palette and recurring red accents that gradually acquire a disturbing significance. Instead of assuming an exact lighting rig, lens or film stock, examine how a selective color motif directs attention across doors, objects and ordinary interiors. Musashino's catalogue identifies Hayashi, Maruo and lighting technician Tomiyama, establishing distinct crew responsibilities without proving every individual creative decision. ([ucla](https://www.international.ucla.edu/cnes/article/33655), [musabi](https://img-lib.musabi.ac.jp/search/document/detail-work/123))

Eye Filmmuseum highlights the opening dial-up modem sound and the grainy computer imagery. An ordinary technological noise becomes part of the film's fictional world and a recognizable auditory sign of its period. As the sound returns in a different dramatic context, familiarity can become unsettling. A learning exercise can use newly created or licensed period-like audio without reproducing the film soundtrack; this formal observation does not establish the original sound hardware, recording format or mix specification. ([eye](https://www.eyefilm.nl/en/whats-on/pulse/1597515), [musabi](https://img-lib.musabi.ac.jp/search/document/detail-work/123), [tiff](https://www.noahcowanfilm.com/japanese-cinema/pulse))

Kurosawa told DVD Talk that a ghost which does very little can be more disturbing to him than a visibly attacking monster. This supports a teaching exercise about held framing, uncertain space and restrained action rather than prescriptive claims that all horror should work this way. The contemporary TIFF programme also situates the film beyond a simple internet-ghost premise. Its genre history should be compared with Cure and Ring without treating three films as a uniform national formula. ([dvd](https://www.dvdtalk.com/interviews/emerging_cinema.html), [tiff](https://www.noahcowanfilm.com/japanese-cinema/pulse), [bfi](https://player.bfi.org.uk/subscription/film/watch-pulse-2001-online))

## Production profile

- **premise:** Design a fictional early-internet ghost story in which empty urban space, ordinary technology and constrained information create unease.
- **screenplay:** Use parallel encounters and partial information to connect mediated contact and social distance.
- **camera:** Preserve the ordinary environment in the frame; introduce selective color accents and ambiguous depth.
- **editing:** Let held moments and incomplete information build dread, rather than explaining each appearance.
- **sound:** Use original or licensed modem-like sounds as period-specific recurring motifs; distinguish sound interpretation from documented equipment.
- **learning:** Distinguish period technology, documented filmmaker intention and critical analysis; keep psychological harm out of scoring.
