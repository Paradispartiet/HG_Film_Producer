# Timecode (2000) — candidate study

Chapter 27 · P2 · Not a canonical production case.

Timecode makes simultaneous viewpoints the central organizing principle of a feature. Four digital cameras record uninterrupted images of intersecting stories; the result places them in adjacent quadrants rather than alternating between them. Figgis described the writing process as analogous to composing music for independent voices: his timeline organized events into minute-long units. This connects the screenplay to coordination of a shared performance, not simply to a split-screen effect. ([dga](https://www.dga.org/Craft/VisualHistory/Interviews/Mike-Figgis), [bfi-live](https://www.bfi.org.uk/sight-and-sound/reviews/timecode-live-quartet-four-cameras))

The method required timed encounters across the different camera groups. During production, reporting in the Los Angeles Times described performers using synchronized watches and improvising within a scheduled outline. Figgis later recalled reviewing complete attempts with cast and crew and spending time refining each group. Accounts differ about the total number of attempts. The important production lesson is collective rehearsal, shared timing and review rather than a supposedly effortless single take. ([lat](https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-1999-nov-08-ca-31187-story.html), [figgis](https://www.splicedwire.com/00features/figgis.html), [wired](https://www.wired.com/2000/04/digital-cinema-plays-with-form/))

The picture construction retains parallel views instead of using conventional cuts to decide what is visible at every moment. Sound provides an alternative means of directing attention: the prominence of a conversation can change even while every picture continues. At a later live screening, Figgis adjusted separate audio elements in real time. This demonstrates a second, exhibition-stage use of the film's structure, not evidence that every original screening had a variable mix. ([bfi-oners](https://www.bfi.org.uk/lists/single-take-films), [bfi-live](https://www.bfi.org.uk/sight-and-sound/reviews/timecode-live-quartet-four-cameras), [rte](https://www.rte.ie/entertainment/2004/0302/401103-figgism/))

The official Sony film record identifies the 2000 Screen Gems release, and Film Fest Gent gives a 97-minute duration and credits Patrick Alexander Stewart for cinematography. IFFR records 98 minutes and a later exhibition format, with inconsistent country/year metadata. These records must remain source-specific. Digital capture, the selected performance, final presentation and subsequent live remix are distinct stages. The available evidence supports a formal production study, not automatic admission into the verified catalogue. ([sony](https://www.sonypictures.com/movies/timecode), [gent](https://www.filmfestival.be/en/film/timecode), [iffr](https://iffr.com/en/iffr/2010/films/time-code))

## Six-phase practice

- **Parallel writing as a score:** Draw four timelines for a three-minute fictional scene with two shared dramatic beats.
- **Ensemble and rehearsal:** Rehearse a short coordinated scene with consenting performers, shared cues and scheduled breaks.
- **Synchronized digital capture:** Plan four short continuous shots with a shared start cue and safe camera routes.
- **Four-panel image construction:** Arrange four original short clips in a grid, keeping their shared timing intact.
- **Sound-led attention and remix:** Create two mixes of the same four-panel scene, shifting dialogue emphasis without altering image timing.
- **Production versions and exhibition:** Write a provenance ledger separating repeated takes, selected version, runtime reports and later live remix.

Reported take counts and runtime discrepancies are retained. No approved representation gap is claimed.
